Itay Kadosh is a PhD student in the Department of Mechanical and Aerospace Engineering at the University of Florida, where he joined the GATAS Lab in 2026. His research interests are robotics, computer vision, and optimization, and in the lab he works on the control and numerical side of CellularSheaves.jl, the package the group uses to pose multi-agent coordination problems as sheaves.

Much of his work there starts from a single observation: coordinating a fleet on a cellular sheaf reduces, at every control step, to one linear system called the harmonic extension, in which a moving target changes only the right-hand side. Itay built the closed-loop model predictive control that drives multi-agent, multi-target tracking problems through that formulation, expressing a tracking scenario as a time-expanded sheaf and solving it as a quadratic program over the resulting trajectory space.

He then attacked the cost of the solve itself. His distributed harmonic extension factors the sheaf Laplacian once, cuts the factor into per-worker slices along its elimination tree, and reduces each control step to a short message-passing protocol, so no machine ever holds the whole problem and the answer stays exact rather than approximate. It builds directly on the multifrontal factorization in CliqueTrees.jl, Richard Samuelson’s package, which is a nice instance of one lab project resting on another. The worked example runs twelve simulated agents across three real processes and matches the centralized answer to about 9 parts in 10 quadrillion; under a fixed half-duplex radio model, the accompanying benchmarks measure it beating both a central coordinator and accelerated diffusion, with the margin growing as the fleet does.

A third strand concerns what sits between the algebra and the vehicles. Writing the agent state as the exact harmonic extension quietly assumes an infinitely fast solver and delivery channel, which leaves no dynamical subsystem for a stability argument to work on. His Tikhonov harmonic tracking introduces an explicit planner state that relaxes onto the harmonic reference at a tunable rate, which exposes the boundary layer that Tikhonov’s theorem needs, quantifies the lag a moving target induces, and identifies the feedforward signal that removes that lag without changing the sheaf, the harmonic objective, or the local controllers. Alongside these he contributed the head-to-head benchmarks comparing diffusion against the direct solve, and set up the package’s GPU continuous integration.

Before joining UF he studied computer science and applied mathematics at the University of Texas at Dallas, where he worked in the Intelligent Robotics and Vision Lab on autonomous exploration and semantic mapping. That work built a robot that maps an indoor environment once and then keeps only the semantic object layer up to date on later visits, so it can track how objects move and change over time without remapping the geometry.
